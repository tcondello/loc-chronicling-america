# Knickerbocker Theatre, January 28, 1922

A motion-picture house in Washington. Saturday night the snow-laden roof buckled, and steel and concrete came down on the audience during a comedy. This is the story as the papers on the Chronicling America shelf told it, from the Monday wires through Edward H. Shaughnessy’s death on Thursday.

Source: [Tim-Pinecone/LOC-Chronicling-America](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Tim-Pinecone/LOC-Chronicling-America). The pages already pulled for this incident, January 30 through February 2, 1922. Six papers carried the disaster. OCR is quoted only where the sentence is clear. Spellings follow the paper named in the sentence.

The count never settled. Monday morning the Ledger’s toll was 108 dead and 132 injured, with the search called off. The same morning Ocala had 107 dead and 133 injured and rescuers still in the wreckage, and Pensacola’s police figure was 107, down from an unofficial peak of 112, with 134 injured. Tuesday the Alaska Daily Empire’s bulletin said the police had fixed the dead at 97, and the story under it said the outside total might not go above 95. Thursday, after Shaughnessy died, both Ocala and the Empire put the police list at 98.

## Saturday night

Ocala calls it a motion-picture theater and says the roof fell “during the showing of a comedy feature bill.” The Empire says family groups had come “to witness the showing of a rollicking comedy,” and that the only cause yet known was “the buckling of the roof.” The Ledger’s Monday lead is the snow-laden roof collapsing Saturday night and “hurling a mass of steel and concrete without warning on the pleasure-seeking hundreds below.” Its picture caption says the ceiling fell in great slabs onto an audience of about 400, and that naval jacks were needed to free some of the bodies. The Omaha Bee’s February 1 caption is a view “looking toward the stage and showing where wreckage crashed down into the orchestra pit,” and an exterior of the street “patrolled by marines.”

The Bee dates the storm as the most terrific snow “in this section in more than 20 years,” and its headline is troops, police, and citizen volunteers taking their lives in their hands in a Washington movie house. Pensacola says the exact number of people in the building will probably never be known. Stores and corridors were turned into first-aid stations. By midnight Sunday the search of the orchestra was down to a far corner. Soldiers, marines, sailors, police, firemen, and volunteers had gone in “without regard to their own risk.” The Empire says one person in the audience saw it coming in time to reach an exit. Several others worked out through a scant opening in the wreckage.

The Bee’s rescue that can be timed is Scott Montgomery. He was brought out at 8:20 Sunday morning after more than eleven hours under an iron girder, given opiates by a doctor who crawled in to him, and died four hours later at Walter Reed. Miss Veronica Murphy, who had come to the theatre with him, was also among the dead. Miss Caroline Upshaw, niece of Representative Upshaw of Georgia, had one foot cut off and the other so mangled the paper said it would have to come off. Ten students at the S.A.E. house had volunteered blood if she needed it.

## Who was in the house

The Empire’s Monday list of people who were there and lived: Senator Ellison D. Smith of South Carolina, and Noble Tomasso, third secretary of the Italian embassy. Representative John H. Smithwick of Florida was among the severely injured. Among the dead in that same dispatch: Oscar Kanston of Chicago, his wife, and two daughters. The fifth of that family, a boy, got out.

Edward H. Shaughnessy, second assistant postmaster general, was injured, and so was his family. The Empire on Monday names Mrs. Shaughnessy and daughters Myrtle and Ruth. Ocala on Thursday says his wife and a ten-year-old daughter, the child with both arms broken and Mrs. Shaughnessy with a fractured rib and shock, and that they had not yet been told he was dead. The Empire the same day says the wife and two daughters were at the bedside when he died that forenoon. Both papers put his death as the one that brought the police list to 98. On Tuesday the Empire had still said he would probably recover.

The Empire’s Thursday sketch of him: ticket agent for the Chicago and North Western at Elgin at fifteen, later superintendent of the railroad, a first lieutenant in the war, superintendent of transportation at Chateau Thierry and at Is-sur-Tille.

Miss Mary A. Forsyth, a Washington resident, died in a hospital on Monday. The Ledger counts her as that day’s death. Miss Helen Dortch, a government employee, was buried Wednesday afternoon at Grace Episcopal in Berryville. The Pensacola Journal says her brother Tyson Dortch, a government tax attorney in New York, and her sister Selina brought the body. It also names their mother, Mrs. Mattie Williams Dortch, and another brother, Allen W. Dortch of Kansas City, Kansas.

The Empire on Wednesday prints this as an additional and partial list, not a finished roll: Scott Montgomery, William A. Walters, D. N. Walsh, Jack McKimie, Christian Thompson, Paul S. Lomby, Miss M. E. Walsh, Kirkland Duke, Esther Foster, Russell Maine, Mrs. Russell Maine, Mrs. Carrie Parsons, Albert Baker, Miss Frances Bikle, Dr. James F. Shea, Miss Mary Forsyth, Archie Bell (formerly of Vineland, New Jersey), Chauncey C. Brainerd, Washington correspondent of the Brooklyn Eagle since 1911 and a former assistant managing editor, and Mrs. Chauncey C. Brainerd, and Wilfred Brosseau of North Adams, Massachusetts, a Georgetown student.

The Bee the day before had already started striking duplicates. Wyatt McKimmie and Jack McKimmie, both carried as dead, were one person. Miss Frances Bikle had been entered twice. Howard W. Kneessi was dead, and a son listed with him was an error. William Walters of Brooklyn, a student, is in that same correction; the Empire’s Wednesday list prints a William A. Walters among the victims.

## Monday through Thursday

Monday the capital was in mourning. The Bee says President and Mrs. Harding were to have been the guests of the Ohio Society that night and that the president asked the reception put off. The Empire says Harding sent his personal physician in. Harry Crandall, manager of the Knickerbocker, kept the five other motion-picture houses of that company closed. He would not take money for amusement while the city was burying its dead. Other theatres opened. By Wednesday the Empire says the theatres were open again and the audiences small. Baron Shidehara, the Japanese ambassador, M. Sarraut of the French arms delegation, and the Danish minister each sent a statement of sorrow.

The Ledger’s Monday line is that the search had been suspended in the belief every body was out, and that any further rise in the 108 would come from a dozen or more still critical. Ocala that morning still had men in the twisted steel and concrete and thought it would be a day before anyone could say the ruins were clear. Pensacola had fourteen of the injured listed as serious. Tuesday the Empire had sixty-five still in hospitals and three of them expected to die, and said a careful recheck, not new rescues, was what pulled the unofficial peak down.

Three inquiries were already talked of on Monday. Ocala says one was ordered by the commissioners of the District of Columbia. The Ledger says a resolution called for an investigation of the Knickerbocker and of other dangerous buildings, and that Hoover’s architects were drafting a standard code for city buildings. The Empire on Tuesday says several District and government agencies had begun, and that Congress might go further. The Pensacola Journal that day says Attorney General Daugherty told reporters the theatre case would delay the grand jury’s Charles W. Morse shipping investigation, because District Attorney Peyton Gordon was starting the theatre inquiry at once.

On Thursday the American Association of Engineers, in the Empire, said the disaster was “evidently” faulty design or construction, and that it would not have happened had licensed engineers or architects supervised the work.

The fear moved faster than the inquest. The Evening World on Monday carries Brooklyn District Attorney Ruston demanding an immediate inspection of every amusement place in New York, and pairing the Knickerbocker with the American Theatre in Brooklyn, which had caved in while still being built and killed seven workmen. The same paper’s Floral Park story says that village board had already refused plans for a picture theatre on Jericho Turnpike because an engineer found the roof too light. The builder, John Hanson, had kept going. The Nassau district attorney told the board he could find nothing in the penal code to stop him.

## What these pages are not

The Evening World on February 1 and 2 prints “KNICKERBOCKER” in the Broadway ads: *Bulldog Drummond* at the Knickerbocker Theatre in New York. The February 2 World also advertises women’s knickerbockers at $6.97. The Ledger of January 31 has Fay’s Knickerbocker bill, a vaudeville and picture program, not the Washington house. None of those is this collapse.
