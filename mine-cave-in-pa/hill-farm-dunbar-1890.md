# Hill Farm mine, June 16, 1890

Dunbar, Fayette County, in the Connellsville coke region. Hill Farm, also printed Farm Hill, was a slope of the Dunbar Furnace Company. This is the story as the papers on the Chronicling America shelf told it, from the first New York wire on the afternoon of the explosion through Superintendent Lang’s arrest in late August.

Source: [Tim-Pinecone/LOC-Chronicling-America](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Tim-Pinecone/LOC-Chronicling-America). Pages with a readable Hill Farm or Farm Hill hit, June 16–August 27, 1890. Ten papers carried it. The Carbon Advocate (Lehighton) and the Chicago Eagle, both publishing that year, did not. The Omaha Daily Bee’s June 17 front page is at the Library of Congress and is not in this dataset’s 1890 folders. Two early hits are other farms: the Sacramento Record-Union on June 9 is Prospect Hill Farm horse sales at Franklin, and the Sun’s June 8 page is not this story. OCR is quoted only where the sentence is clear. Spellings below follow the Pittsburg Dispatch except where another paper is named.

The count never settled, and the mine stayed shut. First wires said 50 or 52 men went in and only 10 came out. By the next morning the Dispatch had 33 cut off in the right heading and two bodies at the surface. Later leads use 31 or 32. The coroner’s jury spoke only for the two men it could see. In mid-August the Dispatch still had the slope on fire, a fan going up to smother it, and the rest of the men unreached. This shelf does not print a later recovery.

## The morning

The shift turned in at 7. A smaller gang went left. The larger one, the Dispatch says some 35 men, took the right drift, down about 800 feet and at least a mile from the mouth. The two drifts met the main stem about half a mile in. The mine had been wet. An air shaft had been drilled from the surface to that junction and had not yet been broken into the workings. The men knew a hole was there. They did not know it was to be opened that day.

John Kerwin had been left in the right drift near the junction. In the course of his work he broke into the perpendicular shaft. Water came first, in a flood. Kerwin and a man named Landy yelled for someone to save the men below, because they thought the right drift would drown. Young David Hays took an open lamp in his hat and went down the left drift through the water to warn them. As he passed the shaft the water changed to firedamp. The lamp flashed the gas. Fire took the nine-foot vein between the main entrance and the right drift and cut those men off. The left drift, fewer than twenty, came out burned and bruised. The Dispatch’s lead that night said 18 of 52 had been in the left heading and got out, and that not one of the right heading escaped.

Pat Kelly, driving a mine car nearby, told the Dispatch the explosion seemed nothing beside the smoke and gas that followed him to the shaft door and poured out over the hill. By midnight two bodies were up. The same paper’s narrative says old David Hays, father of the boy with the lamp, went into the smoke after his son and was drawn out about an hour later with James Shearn, recognized by their wives. The July 12 verdict names those two bodies David Hay and Daniel Shearin: Hay died trying to rescue his boy, Shearin died as fire boss, both suffocated by afterdamp and smoke.

The Cambria Freeman puts the slope at Pechin, a Baltimore and Ohio station about a quarter mile west of the mine and about 64 miles from Pittsburgh. The entrance was a drift or slope descending about 4,000 feet. Nearby workings used in the rescue were the Mahoning mine (the Sun says a mile and a half, and spells it Mahonoy), the Ferguson mine, and the Anchor mines.

Clocks in the first wires: Johnstown’s weekly says 10 o’clock, the Cambria Freeman says 11:07, Fort Worth and the Hocking Sentinel say 11:10, and the Dispatch says shortly before noon. The Evening World’s first telegram is OCR-dated June 10. Monday, and every later “explosion of June 16,” fixes the day.

Peter Egan was working at the lowest point. On June 23 his son told the Evening World, in tears, that he had given his father up. The lowest slope had filled with water. The boy said he would never go into a mine. His father had not allowed him to. Miners were saying bad luck had followed the place since the death of an old miner called Mike the Gougher. John Tehaney said that the night before the disaster he saw a hoary cloud over the pit mouth and would not enter on Monday. That decision, the paper says, saved his life.

## Two weeks at the pit mouth

By the evening of June 16 some experts had already given the men up. The Evening World on June 17 said the thirty-one died by fire and suffocation, and that the night work was only to reach bodies. The Dispatch the next morning still ran “SOME MAY YET LIVE.” Funerals of the two recovered men were set for that day. Smoke thinned, then thickened again once volunteers opened seventy feet in the Mahoning and the draft into Hill Farm jumped. The paper’s figures for that draft are 16,000 to 29,000 cubic feet.

Martin Markov’s crawl on the morning of June 18 is the Sun’s scene: from the Mahoning entrance, a mile and a half over fallen slate, to within ten yards of the fatal chambers, calling, with no answer. At noon a rescuer reported a mule braying deep in the slope, and the crowd took that as a sign of life. Trains kept bringing people up the hill.

June 19 the Dispatch had hope fading, a rumor that Inspector Keighley had already condemned the burning mine, and the police doubled for fear of an outbreak if the men were found dead. June 20 flipped the headline to hope revived: faint sounds, “LIVE MEN ARE THERE,” the separating wall nearly pierced, hundreds of men and women at the mouth. June 21 was a day of suspense. The tappings were still heard, and a miner of long experience said they were falling coal. The Evening World that day names “noble John Jenkins,” who labored until he sank, fatally overcome. The page does not say who Jenkins was beyond that.

On June 22 four signals of the engineer’s bell came from inside. Falling slate or a pulled wire could both make them. No answering taps followed, and a fresh explosion was already a fear for the rescuers. Damage suits were being talked about. June 23 a heavy fall of coal reset the cut. Colonel Hill, who had owned the mine before he sold it to the Dunbar Furnace Company, said they should have been through the solid coal days earlier and that the maps were imperfect. The same day the Evening World has the lowest slope full of water, and Peter Egan’s son giving up. Fort Worth that evening carried an appeal: seven days out, the families destitute.

June 24 at about 3:30 a.m. a hole was broken through coal and bratticed with muslin. Johnstown’s weekly reprints the rush of newspaper men and physicians up the hill. The next day’s Dispatch said the report that the mine had been entered was unfounded. June 26, the tenth day, the most sanguine said the men must be dead. Forty-eight men were still digging, and the rescuers were at sea about where they were. The same issue denounces the owners and says the disaster could have been averted. The editorial argues that a store of food left for entombed miners would have repaid its cost across the whole region.

June 27 the papers thought they were feet away, this time by a heading from the Ferguson mine, and that only two men would be allowed into the chamber when the wall went. A Sacramento wire datelined June 20, printed June 27, says a gang had been quietly cutting from Ferguson for two days and was stopped when they were close, until someone could say whether gas sat in that tunnel. At 8:30 that night the fire that had been burning inside came out the mouth. The Sun: flames thirty feet, nearby buildings torn down so the fire would not spread, inspectors and District Master Workman Watchorn debating whether to abandon the search. The Evening World the next day calls the column of black smoke, then the flame, the pall over the last hope for the thirty-one, and says the rescuers failed by misdirection.

June 29 the four men who went into Mahoning came out without having pierced Farm Hill. The hole was a crevice. The maps, they said, were wrong, and they were as much in the dark as on the first day, thirteen days in. June 30 the fire at the mouth had subsided and the inspectors gave up finding anyone alive. Keighley said the inquest would fix the blame. A letter from J. G. G. of Baden, dated June 28, asked why mines were not required to drill numbered holes from the surface into rooms and entries, so people above could find entombed men, feed them, and give them air.

## They got in

They did get in, in the first days of July, and the mine drove them out. The Dispatch’s July 3 editorial: three explorers went 1,800 feet on Tuesday, through ground that had been smoke and black damp until the Mahoning fans moved the gas; they found coats and dinner buckets dropped in haste. The Sun: Inspector Keighley, Secretary Watchorn, and thirteen others went down the slope, crumbling roofs and firedamp, and found picks, dinner pails, coats, and a mule that had suffocated. No miners. They would not go back. The Evening World: nearly 700 feet, the fire working toward the entrance, a wagon-load of coffins that would go unused, bodies probably never recovered. The Dispatch had counted 30 coffins hauled to the manway in the night and carried below, then the search ended.

The Cambria Freeman: the air at 4 a.m. tested 20 percent black damp; Superintendent Hill took that as proof no one was alive; nine men including Watchorn went back in; at 2 p.m. Dr. Walker came out and said all thirty-one were dead, the smoke too dense to survive ten feet past the break, two buckets and coats found, the men in the slope burned. Sacramento, datelined July 1: rescuers satisfied all were dead, the smell of burning flesh almost overpowering, the work of smothering the fire begun, the mine impossible to flood. The Hocking Sentinel: hope and work abandoned at 6 p.m., the dust or ashes of the thirty-one left under Dunbar hill, the caskets carried back down.

## Sealing, the inquest, and the charge

July 2 the Dispatch’s staff correspondent watched every known opening being closed. Keighley had a brick wall three feet thick built in the Mahoning cut and left for Uniontown. The two bore holes were plugged. Brick brattices went into the Ferguson entrance and the Hill Farm manway, which ran parallel to the main slope and twenty feet from it. Thin blue smoke, more heat than smoke, kept anyone from within forty or fifty feet of the pit mouth. An engine forced compressed air down the manway so workmen could reach 500 feet and wall it. The company had 120 men at Hill Farm and the ovens, and 80 at Ferguson; it hoped to put them back to work within a week or two. An appeal went out for the widows and orphans of the thirty-two. A Democratic resolution the same week sympathized with the relatives and demanded more mine-safety law. Scottdale’s Methodist church, under Rev. Dr. Connor, had already sent $108.25.

The inquest opened at Dunbar. An attempt to move it to Uniontown failed. On the afternoon of July 8, printed July 9, John Kerwin told the rush of water and the flash of gas. The room stayed still, and an audible sigh went up when he finished. He said Hay shouted “Is my boy down the slope?” and plunged into the fire and smoke. Mr. Downs of the Knights of Labor aimed questions at Inspector Keighley. Mr. Lindsay, for the Dunbar company, fought any inquiry into how the rescue itself had been run. Keighley admitted he had told a miners’ committee he would close the mine if he found it as bad as they said, and that after his examination it was worse. Robert Lang, first witness that morning, said Hill Farm and Ferguson were operated as one mine, and that the drill hole was tapped at his instance.

July 10, miners testified that gas had been in Farm Hill, that Keighley knew it, and that promised improvements were not done. Secretary Watchorn said neglect caused the accident, and that another opening would have kept it from happening. July 16, jury foreman ’Squire Cotton said the verdict followed the evidence, that someone was responsible, and that Lang gave the orders. One juror had wanted to change his mind on the second day they sat; Cotton would not say what the change was. The same day the United Mine Workers executive board (Watchorn, Patrick McBride, William Scaife of Illinois, W. C. Webb of Kentucky, John Kane of Indiana, E. F. Warren of Pennsylvania) noted that 23 of the 32 dead were members in good standing and voted to bring damage suits in the names of the widows and orphans at $10,000 each. The paragraph also says 31 lives were lost.

### The verdict

Coroner James F. Holbert of Fayette County held the finding 21 hours, then gave it out at Uniontown on the evening of July 11. The inquisition was taken at Dunbar borough on July 10, upon the view of the bodies of David Hay and Daniel Shearin. The jury was W. H. Cottom, Samuel Moyer, William Trew, William Blacka, Michael Reagan, and John Lowe. They found that Hay and Shearin died June 16 in the Hill Farm mine in the discharge of duty, Hay trying to rescue his boy and Shearin as fire boss, suffocated by afterdamp and smoke from an explosion of gas caused by John Kerwin opening a drill hole in an unlawful manner under the directions and by order of Superintendent Robert Lang, and that Lang was criminally responsible for those two deaths.

Freeland on July 17 spells him Robert Long. Sacramento on July 12 spells him Robert Ling. The arrest item’s OCR reads Liang. One man.

### Hay, alive

On July 25 in Connellsville, John Goodwin and Jake Wood, Hill Farm miners who had been on the rescuing party, gave the Dispatch a different death for David Hay. Wood said Hay was found alive and kicking, and would have lived if he had been brought into fresh air, and that the men in charge did not know what to do. He said Mine Boss Evans pressed him to work when the gas was too bad, sent him home when he refused, then put him farther down, and that gas came through from the Ferguson half the time while men worked with open lights. Goodwin said he had been told the same about Hay, and that old miners knew to bury a man in wet clay so the clay would draw choke-damp out of the body. Both said Hay was not brought out at once because someone thought it would create too much excitement. The coroner had already viewed Hay’s body. The paper prints the claim as a sensation, five weeks after the funerals of the two recovered men.

## August

The Parrich plant of the Dunbar Furnace Company, idle since the disaster, resumed in the last week of July. On August 8 Mahoning and Hill Farm were still out and the fire still raged. Scottdale, August 14: still ablaze, every attempt to extinguish it futile, a large fan erected and the mine closed, the flames expected to take months. The families, that wire says, were provided for, with over $10,000 collected.

On the morning of August 25, Mrs. James Smith of Dunbar, widow of one of the buried miners, swore an information before ’Squire Wilson in Uniontown charging Lang with criminal negligence in her husband’s death. Twelve other widows and Master Workman Kerfoot of the Knights of Labor went with her. The next night’s Dispatch, datelined Scottdale, August 26: the mine still on fire, the loss already in the thousands, the fan frame nearly done, and Superintendent Lang was arrested the night before for criminal negligence. The charges were preferred by the widows. He gave bail for court.

The shelf ends there. The fan was not yet in the pit. The men on the roll were still in the hill.
